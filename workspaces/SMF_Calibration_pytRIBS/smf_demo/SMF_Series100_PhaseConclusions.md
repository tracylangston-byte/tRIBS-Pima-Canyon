# SMF Series 100 Family — Phase Conclusions: Parameter Recovery & Basin Transferability

**Prepared for:** Tracy Langston, Summer 2026 SMF calibration phase
**Covers:** A 400-run Latin Hypercube Sample (LHS) sweep of saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ks) and its depth-decay rate (f) against a known synthetic truth, plus three siblings of that same sweep run against the same truth at 0.8×, 1.0×, and 1.25× rainfall intensity — all for the South Mountain Fan (SMF) watershed, August 12, 2014 convective storm event.

## 0. What this document is answering

Two overarching questions sit behind this whole phase of work: **can this modeling approach recover known parameters from a synthetic event, and what does that imply for applying it to a real basin that hasn't been calibrated yet?** Both break down into more specific, separately-answerable pieces:

1. **Point recovery:** given a single known event, does the best-scoring run in a parameter sweep land on (or near) the true parameters?
2. **Structural identifiability:** independent of whether the *best* run is close to truth, how much of parameter space is *consistent* with the observed hydrograph — a point, a small region, or something larger?
3. **Multi-event identifiability:** does using more than one event (here, the same storm scaled to three magnitudes) shrink that region, and toward the true parameters or away from them?
4. **Transferability:** what does an answer to 1–3 — built on a single training basin, with a synthetic (and therefore noise-free) truth — actually tell you about calibrating a real, currently-ungauged basin?

The short version: **1–3 have clean, well-evidenced answers. 4 is the honest open question** — this phase built the methodology and evidence base that answering it will need, but it hasn't been tested on a second basin yet, and shouldn't be oversold as "transferability confirmed" on the strength of single-basin results alone.

**A methods note used throughout this document:** all the parameter-recovery work described here relies on scoring a simulated hydrograph against a *synthetic* truth — a hydrograph generated by running the model once with known, chosen parameters, then treating that output as if it were the real observed record. The advantage of a synthetic truth over a real gauge record is that the right answer is known in advance, so it's possible to directly check whether a calibration procedure actually recovers it, rather than only checking whether it produces a plausible-looking fit. Every "true parameters" reference below means the parameter values used to generate that synthetic record, not anything measured in the field.

## 1. Bottom line

- **Yes, this model setup recovers the true parameters of a synthetic storm — under noise-free, single-basin, correctly-scored conditions.** The best-scoring run in the 400-run baseline sweep lands at Ks=6.957× the baseline multiplier, f=0.0121, against a true value of Ks=7.0×, f=0.012 (composite score 0.994 out of a perfect 1.0). That's about as clean a validation as a noise-free synthetic test can give.
- **But "recovers" means "the best-scoring point is near truth," not "only points near truth fit well."** The set of acceptable parameter combinations traces a curved band in (Ks, f) space — not a single point. Near the true f (~0.012) that band is at its *widest* (most forgiving), spanning roughly 4 units of Ks-multiplier worth of nearly-as-good alternatives. This is real ambiguity in what the data can tell you, not scoring noise.
- **Decomposing that single event's fit score into its separate parts does more work than the width measurement alone suggests.** The band traced by matching total flow *volume* actually bends back on itself at high f into a second, disconnected region that would look like a competitive alternative to the true parameters if volume were all that mattered. A separate component of the fit score — one that measures how well the *shape* of the hydrograph matches, independent of its total volume — rules that entire region out, not just narrows it. This is described in full in §2.2.
- **Combining three storm magnitudes of the same event narrows the acceptable region by roughly 8×** relative to any single storm's own acceptable region, and — critically, only once a scoring bug described in §2.1 was fixed — the narrowed, combined region actually contains the true parameters, which it did not before the fix. That's a meaningful, load-bearing result: multi-event calibration is doing real identifiability work here, not just producing a smaller region that happens to miss the answer.
- **This is all still a single-basin, zero-noise result.** It validates the *workflow* — sweep parameters, score against a known truth, identify the best-fitting region — on the basin where the answer is already known and under the best possible conditions. It does not yet say anything about a real, currently-ungauged basin, where the truth is unknown and the data will carry gauge error, spatial rainfall uncertainty, and model structural error that this phase deliberately excluded by design.

## 2. Can this approach recover the true parameters of a synthetic storm?

### 2.1 Single-event recovery: yes, cleanly, once scored correctly

A 400-run LHS sweep across Ks-multiplier and f (all other parameters held fixed at their true values) was scored against a synthetic truth generated at Ks=7.0×, f=0.012. The best-scoring run:

| | Ks-multiplier | f | Composite score | Volume bias (PBIAS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **best-scoring run** | 6.957 | 0.01212 | 0.9937 | +0.56% |
| true parameters | 7.0 | 0.012 | — | — |

**This result only exists because of a scoring bug that had to be found and fixed first, and it's worth explaining because the same category of bug could recur at a new basin.** Comparing a simulated hydrograph to an observed one requires putting both time series on the same time grid — here, resampling both to a common 5-minute interval. The original scoring pipeline resampled the *observed* series by interpolating between points, but resampled the *simulated* series by averaging within each 5-minute window instead. Those two approaches don't agree, especially for a sharply peaked flash-flood hydrograph, and the mismatch introduced a systematic bias into every accuracy metric — worse for larger, faster-changing storms. It wasn't obvious from the numbers alone that anything was wrong, because the biased scores still behaved sensibly relative to each other (better-fitting runs still scored better than worse-fitting ones). It only became visible when the pipeline was tested at the one point where the right answer is known in advance: scoring the true parameters against themselves should give a perfect score, and instead gave a composite score well below 1.0. Under that buggy scoring, the apparent best run was Ks=7.836×, f=0.0191 — a real miss, not just a noisier estimate of the right answer. After the fix, the best run moved to Ks=6.957×/f=0.0121, essentially on top of the truth — landing on the right answer rather than some other, still-wrong point is itself evidence the fix was correct.

The ten best-scoring runs cluster along a diagonal band from (Ks≈4.2, f≈0.005) to (Ks≈7.6, f≈0.013) — consistent with a band of acceptable combinations, not an isolated peak:

| Ks-multiplier | f | Composite score | Volume bias (PBIAS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.957 | 0.01212 | 0.9937 | +0.56% |
| 7.453 | 0.01286 | 0.9936 | +0.63% |
| 6.126 | 0.00977 | 0.9896 | −0.33% |
| 5.096 | 0.00771 | 0.9895 | +0.56% |
| 4.913 | 0.00706 | 0.9883 | −0.64% |

### 2.2 The acceptable region is a band, not a point — and one fit-score component eliminates an entire branch of it

This is the most important finding from the baseline sweep, and it has two layers. The first is that the band's width — how forgiving the trade-off between Ks and f is — varies along its length. The second, more consequential layer is that the band isn't even the whole picture: splitting the composite fit score into its component parts reveals that two of them disagree about which parts of the band are trustworthy, and one throws out an entire branch that the other would happily accept.

**Background — how the composite fit score is built, and why it was changed partway through this phase.** The metric used throughout ("KGE," Kling-Gupta Efficiency) is a single number built by combining three separate comparisons between simulated and observed flow: how well the *timing pattern* correlates (r), how well the *total volume* matches (a bias ratio, called beta — the same information as percent volume bias, PBIAS, in different units), and how well the *variability* matches (originally a ratio of standard deviations, called alpha, under the metric's original 2009 formulation). The problem with the original formulation, discovered partway through this phase, is that alpha and beta turn out to be nearly redundant in this dataset — alpha and beta are correlated at r=0.993, meaning alpha's score is almost entirely predictable from beta's score alone and isn't contributing independent information about anything beyond volume. A 2012 revision of the same metric replaces alpha with a related but different quantity, gamma — the ratio of *coefficient of variation* (standard deviation divided by mean) between simulated and observed, rather than the raw standard-deviation ratio. Dividing out the mean before comparing variability is exactly what decouples this term from volume: in this dataset, gamma correlates with beta at only r=0.368, meaning it's actually carrying separate information about the *shape* of the hydrograph, independent of how much total water it moves. This project switched to the 2012 formulation for that reason. The switch doesn't change any of this document's headline findings — the composite-score ranking under the old and new formulations correlates at r=0.994, and the single best-scoring run is identical either way — it just makes decomposing "did volume match" from "did shape match" actually mean something.

![KGE 2009 vs 2012 formulation comparison](../calibration_work/03_comparisons/sensitivity_plots/kge2009_vs_2012_comparison.png)

*Left: alpha vs. beta under the original formulation — nearly a straight line (r=0.993), meaning alpha isn't adding independent information. Middle: gamma vs. beta under the revised formulation — decoupled (r=0.368). Right: the composite score is nearly identical either way (r=0.994), so nothing about the headline "where's the best run" conclusion depends on which formulation is used.*

**Layer 1 — the band's width varies with f, and peaks almost exactly at the true f.** Measuring the width of the acceptable band (defined as combinations scoring within 0.2 of the best possible composite score) at several fixed values of f:

| f | ridge peak Ks | peak composite score | band width (Ks-multiplier units) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.006 | 4.39 | 0.957 | 2.60 |
| 0.008 | 5.23 | 0.988 | 3.24 |
| 0.010 | 6.19 | 0.989 | 3.76 |
| **0.012 (≈true f)** | **6.93** | **0.993** | **3.98 (widest)** |
| 0.015 | 8.05 | 0.958 | 3.20 |
| 0.020 | 8.19 | 0.916 | 1.52 |
| 0.030 | 7.17 | 0.830 | 0.66 (narrowest) |

Physically: near the true depth-decay rate, Ks and f can trade off freely — a somewhat-too-low Ks can be compensated by a somewhat-too-high f and vice versa, and the model can't distinguish the difference from the hydrograph alone. That flexibility disappears at high f.

![Figure 9: ridge width vs f](../calibration_work/03_comparisons/sensitivity_plots/lhs_Ks_f_100/fig9_ridge_width_vs_f.png)

*Figure 9 — panel A shows the cross-sections this table is measured from; panel B is the width-vs-f curve itself, peaking right at the true f and collapsing by a factor of ~6 past f≈0.02.*

**Layer 2 — the volume-only band actually bends back at high f into a second, disconnected region — and the shape-matching component eliminates it completely, not just partially.** Trace the zero-volume-bias line in Figure 1, or the pale-blue "volume-matched" region in the bottom row of Figure 10 below, and it doesn't behave like a single band extending indefinitely. It climbs from low Ks/low f up to about Ks≈8.2 by f≈0.017–0.02, then bends back, curving toward *lower* Ks as f keeps climbing, reaching all the way up to f≈0.048 at Ks≈5.5–6. This isn't an artifact of interpolating between sampled points — it's confirmed against one of the actual, individually-sampled LHS draws: the run at Ks=5.750×/f=0.04626 achieves +3.0% volume bias (essentially volume-matched) but its shape-match score is 0.798 (badly damped relative to observed) and its composite score is 0.795, both clearly poor. Judged on volume alone, that run would look like a second, competitive candidate for the true parameters, sitting on a completely different part of the parameter space from the actual answer.

The shape-matching component rules this second region out categorically. Measured directly on the same interpolated surfaces Figure 10 is built from: of all the grid cells where volume is matched (within 2% bias), 23% sit at f > 0.017 — that's the high-f branch just described. **Zero percent of those cells are also shape-matched** (within the same 2% tolerance applied to the shape term). This isn't sensitive to exactly where the branch is judged to start — cutoffs from f > 0.015 through f > 0.025 all show 0% joint survival; only right at the transition (f > 0.014) does a small sliver (5.5%) still pass both criteria. The shape-matching component isn't narrowing this branch — it's eliminating it.

**Why volume and shape disagree here:** volume trades off cleanly and indefinitely between Ks and f — more Ks needs proportionally more f to move the same total amount of water — which is exactly the relationship that bends back on itself once f gets large enough that very little additional Ks is needed to compensate. Shape-matching doesn't share that flexibility: it degrades with f regardless of whether Ks is correctly compensating for volume, and past some point, no Ks value fixes it.

![Figure 4: gamma (shape-matching) contour](../calibration_work/03_comparisons/sensitivity_plots/lhs_Ks_f_100/fig4_gamma_contour.png)

*Figure 4 — the shape-matching term's own "good" region is a closed island toward low Ks/low-to-mid f, not an open band like the volume term — it simply doesn't extend into the high-f branch at all.*

A further check, restricting the sweep to the 16 runs already volume-matched (within 3%) and asking whether shape-match still degrades with f *within that subset* — i.e., among runs that already have the right total volume, does shape hold up, or fail independently?

| f (volume-matched runs only) | n runs | mean shape score | mean composite score |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.003–0.007 | 4 | 0.993 | 0.976 |
| 0.007–0.010 | 4 | 1.009 | 0.988 |
| 0.010–0.013 | 5 | 1.000 | 0.989 |
| 0.013–0.017 | 2 | 0.999 | 0.978 |
| 0.017–0.022 | 1 | 0.913 | 0.912 |

Shape-match stays close to 1.0 (perfect) through f≈0.013, then drops to 0.91 by f≈0.02 — even among runs where volume is already correct. The sample is thin (only one point above f≈0.017), so on its own this is corroborating rather than conclusive — but it converges with two independent checks: the raw-sample evidence above, and the interpolated width table's own peak-score column degrading on the same schedule.

**A closely related question worth addressing directly: near the very best-scoring runs, is the timing-correlation component (r) — the third piece of the composite score, alongside volume and shape — actually adding real information, or could small, physically meaningless differences in r be deciding which run gets called "best"?** This matters because the routing parameters that actually control timing (see §2.5) were held fixed throughout this sweep, so any spread in r among top runs isn't real information about Ks or f — it would be closer to noise. Checked directly: among the 20 best-scoring runs, r has by far the smallest spread of the three components (standard deviation 0.0025, versus 0.010 for shape and 0.023 for volume), and its correlation with overall fit quality is small and in the sensible direction (−0.207), not some artifact. r is not acting as a tiebreaker.

**Why this matters more than "the band narrows":** volume and shape fail for different, separable reasons. Volume is rescuable by trading Ks against f — indefinitely, in principle, which is exactly what produces the bent-back high-f branch. Shape is not rescuable the same way. This means **decomposing a single event's fit score into its parts, and multi-event combination (§2.3), are two independent, stackable ways to narrow what's identifiable** — not two versions of the same trick. One comes from combining separate observations; the other comes from noticing that a single event's own composite score is hiding a disagreement between its parts. The top row of Figure 10 makes one more thing visible: a version of the composite score built from only the volume and shape terms, with the timing-correlation term left out entirely, looks almost identical to Figure 1 — meaning the timing-correlation term isn't rescuing or obscuring any of this; the whole story is in volume and shape.

![Figure 10: KGE without r, and the literal volume/shape intersection](../calibration_work/03_comparisons/sensitivity_plots/lhs_Ks_f_100/fig10_beta_gamma_intersection.png)

*Figure 10 — top row: the composite score built from volume and shape alone, without the timing-correlation term, compared to Figure 1. Bottom row: the volume-matched region (blue) and shape-matched region (orange) overlaid directly — the dark overlap is where both hold, and it stops exactly where the high-f branch begins.*

### 2.3 Multi-event recovery: three storm magnitudes narrow the band by ~8×, and now actually bracket the truth

The same sweep design was repeated three times against the same true parameters, with only the rainfall intensity of the forcing storm scaled — 0.8×, 1.0×, and 1.25× the original event — to test whether combining evidence from multiple event magnitudes does better than relying on any single one.

| storm | rainfall scale | best composite score | best-scoring run (Ks, f) | distance from truth (Ks units) | curvature of the acceptable band | fraction of grid volume-matched |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.8× | 0.80 | 0.9940 | (7.520, 0.01409) | 0.520 | −8.45 | 2.43% |
| 1.0× (matched bounds) | 1.00 | 0.9896 | (4.778, 0.00679) | 2.222 | −11.41 | 3.70% |
| 1.25× | 1.25 | 0.9946 | (5.691, 0.00905) | 1.309 | −13.01 | 6.23% |

**Combined (all three simultaneously, volume-matched within 2%): 0.298% of the parameter grid — about 8× tighter than the narrowest single storm's own acceptable region** (0.298/2.43 ≈ 0.123). And unlike before the resampling bug described in §2.1 was fixed, this combined region now genuinely contains the true parameters — a materially stronger claim than "the region got smaller."

Two things worth flagging directly, since they complicate a simple story:

- **The acceptable band curves more sharply as storm magnitude increases** (curvature coefficient −8.45 → −11.41 → −13.01 across the three storms), consistent with the idea that f governs how quickly infiltration capacity decays with depth, and a bigger storm drives a deeper wetting front, tracing a more curved trade-off. This is an *interpretive* claim consistent with the data, not a proven mechanism.
- **A counterintuitive nuance: the best-scoring run for a given storm isn't necessarily the run closest to the true parameters, and this gets more pronounced at higher storm magnitude.** The 0.8× storm's best run is quite close to truth (distance 0.52); the 1.0× and 1.25× storms' best runs are farther (2.22 and 1.31) even though their composite scores are just as good or better. A high fit score doesn't automatically mean "close to true parameters" — it means "consistent with the observed hydrograph," and at larger storms more of the acceptable band can produce a near-perfect hydrograph match while still being parametrically wrong. This is exactly the kind of thing multi-event calibration is supposed to fix, and the combined-region number above shows it does — but it's a caution against reading any single storm's best-scoring run as a parameter estimate on its own.

### 2.4 Robustness check: none of this depends on which version of the composite score is used

A separate piece of due diligence: the original (2009) and revised (2012) formulations of the composite score were compared directly across all four datasets in this family. The headline findings above — the sweet spot near Ks≈7×, recovery of the true parameters, the acceptable band's curvature increasing with storm magnitude — are unaffected by the choice: composite-score correlation between the two formulations is r=0.995–0.999 across all four datasets, the top-20 rankings overlap 17–19 out of 20, and the single best-scoring run is identical under both formulations in every case. The formula choice does matter for one specific thing, already described in §2.2: the original formulation's variability term is ~99% redundant with its volume term, while the revised formulation's decouples to r≈0.2–0.4 — which is why the revised formulation is used for the decomposition work in §2.2, even though it doesn't change any headline number in this document.

### 2.5 Routing parameters (cv, r, n): a different identifiability story, established before this phase

Everything above concerns Ks and f, which jointly control how much water the hillslopes generate and roughly how it decays with depth — in effect, the volume and coarse shape of the flood. Three other parameters control routing — how that water travels once generated: a hillslope-flow velocity coefficient (referred to here as cv), a hillslope-flow velocity exponent (referred to here as r — unrelated to the timing-correlation r discussed above; the naming collision is unfortunate but both terms are standard usage in their respective contexts), and a channel roughness coefficient (referred to here as n). These three were held fixed at their true values throughout every sweep described above. Their own identifiability was established in earlier work this project built on, not repeated fresh here — but since it directly shapes how a real calibration should be structured, it's summarized below with the evidence behind it.

**The workflow used to establish this, reproducible at a new basin:**

1. Fix Ks and f at a volume-matched point (any point along the acceptable band described in §2.2 will do, since volume is what needs to be pinned down first).
2. With Ks and f fixed, run a parameter sweep over cv, r, and n only, scored against a synthetic truth.
3. **Evaluate with metrics that isolate specific phases of the hydrograph, not the single composite score.** The composite score turns out to be a poor tool for this — it doesn't discriminate cv or n at all, and shows a real but confusing behavior for r (see below). The metrics that do the discriminating instead: how long the model takes to first produce any flow at all (first arrival error), how steeply flow rises once it starts (rising limb steepness), how long it takes to go from first flow to peak (time-to-peak-from-exceedance), how far off the peak magnitude is, how far off the peak's timing is, how long flow stays above a threshold (duration above threshold), and how quickly flow recedes after the peak (recession rate, measured as the slope of the falling limb on a log scale).

**Result, established across several rounds of this sweep and confirmed to hold at multiple different volume-matched Ks/f points (not just the true one):**

- **Ks and f own volume** — already covered in §2.1–2.2.
- **r owns hydrograph shape**, most clearly visible in the recession-rate metric (a strong, consistent relationship across every round this was tested) and to a lesser degree in peak discharge and rising-limb steepness.
- **n owns routing-speed timing — but only visible through the timing-specific metrics, not through the composite score at all.** Specifically, it shows up in time-to-peak-from-exceedance and peak timing error, which isolate how fast water routes through the channel network once it's already flowing, separate from when the storm itself arrives.
- **cv is genuinely equifinal — not just weak, but essentially unconstrained by every metric tested, including the timing-specific ones that successfully pin down n.** This isn't a case of "cv matters but the composite score can't see it" the way n's story goes; cv doesn't show a strong signal anywhere in this evidence base.

**A methodological trap worth flagging on its own, because it's easy to reproduce accidentally at a new basin:** in an earlier round of this same test, n *looked* unidentifiable — a genuine null result, as far as the analysis at the time could tell. That turned out to be wrong: the model's output was only being saved once per hour, which is too coarse to resolve the timing differences n actually produces, and the sampled range of n values was too narrow to see its effect clearly either. Once both the output resolution (down to 5-minute intervals) and the sampled range were widened, n's real signal in the timing-specific metrics appeared clearly. **A false "this parameter isn't identifiable" conclusion can be a resolution or range artifact, not a real finding — this should be checked explicitly (fine output resolution, a range wide enough to see an effect) before concluding any routing parameter is truly unconstrained.**

**A second nuance worth keeping:** r's relationship with the *composite* score specifically shows an inconsistent, "branch"-like pattern that doesn't trace back to any of the score's individual components when checked separately — an artifact of how the composite score combines its parts nonlinearly, not a physical effect. This is a second, independent reason to prefer the phase-specific metrics over the composite score when trying to identify r and n: the composite score can behave in ways that don't reflect anything physically real.

**An important caveat on the specific numbers above:** the illustrative correlation magnitudes behind these findings (recession rate vs. r around ρ≈−0.88; timing metrics vs. n around r≈0.6–0.7) come from rounds of this test that used an earlier version of the synthetic truth, not the Ks=7.0×/f=0.012 truth used everywhere else in this document, and the raw per-run data behind them no longer exists to re-verify (removed in a disk cleanup during this project). The **qualitative hierarchy — Ks/f own volume, r owns shape, n owns timing-only-via-timing-metrics, cv is genuinely equifinal — is well-established, having been confirmed across several independent rounds of testing, including a check that it holds at multiple different Ks/f anchor points, not just the true one.** The specific numbers should be treated as illustrative of the size of the effect, not as current, re-verified figures. If any of this needs to be cited as a hard number rather than a directional finding, it should be re-run against the current truth first.

**What this means for calibration strategy, here or at a new basin:** don't try to calibrate cv, r, and n by optimizing the composite score — it won't discriminate cv or n at all, and can mislead about r. Instead, fix Ks/f first using the volume-matching approach in §2.1–2.2, then evaluate cv/r/n using phase-specific timing metrics computed at fine output resolution over a wide enough sampled range, and expect cv specifically to remain unconstrained regardless of how that's done — it should be set from a reasonable prior value rather than calibrated, since the data doesn't support pinning it down.

## 3. What does this say about basin transferability?

### 3.1 What's actually been tested, and what hasn't

Every result in §2 was produced under conditions specifically designed to be the *best case* for parameter recovery: a single training basin, a synthetic (not real) truth, zero observation noise, zero forcing-data uncertainty beyond the deliberately-varied storm magnitude, and (once the resampling bug from §2.1 was found) a scoring pipeline independently verified to reproduce a perfect score exactly at the true parameter point. **None of this has been tested on a second basin, and none of it has been tested with real noise.** That's not a shortcoming of this phase — it's what a controlled methodology check is supposed to do before spending compute and trust on a real, currently-less-well-characterized basin — but it means transferability hasn't been directly demonstrated yet. What this phase demonstrates is closer to: *the tool and the calibration workflow are trustworthy enough, on the basin where the answer can be checked, to be worth pointing at the basin where it can't.*

### 3.2 What this phase de-risks for the next basin

Even without a direct cross-basin test, several things carry over in a genuine, not just rhetorical, way:

- **The workflow itself is validated end-to-end.** Sweep parameters → score against a known truth → identify the best-fitting region is now a proven pipeline that recovers a known answer to within 0.6% of Ks and 0.9% of f. That's the harness the next basin's calibration will reuse.
- **The identifiability structure of every free parameter in this class of arid, infiltration-excess-dominated basin is now mapped, not just Ks and f.** Ks/f are strongly identifiable jointly from a single event's composite score (and further narrowed by decomposing that score, per §2.2). The three routing parameters have a completely different profile — r and n identifiable only via phase-specific metrics rather than the composite score, and cv genuinely unconstrained regardless of method (§2.5). That division of labor is a general statement about what a single-outlet hydrograph can and can't tell you, and there's no obvious reason to expect it to reverse at a new basin — though it should be re-confirmed there, not assumed.
- **The acceptable-region structure itself is a transferable warning.** Even a "successful" single-basin calibration should be expected to under-report its own uncertainty if reported as a single point estimate. Any parameter set transferred to, or fit fresh at, a new basin should be reported as a *range*, not a single number, unless multi-event calibration has been used to narrow it the way §2.3 does here.
- **Decomposing a composite fit score into its parts, rather than reading it as one number, is a de-risking practice with zero marginal data cost.** §2.2 showed that a volume-only view of this same sweep would have accepted an entire spurious high-f solution region that the shape-matching component rules out completely. That's not specific to this basin — it's a general caution about calibrating on a composite score (especially the original 2009 formulation, where its components are nearly redundant) without checking whether its parts actually agree.
- **The methodological practices this phase had to (re)learn are themselves the most literally transferable output** — more so than any specific parameter value. See §4.

### 3.3 What doesn't transfer, or is still genuinely open

- **Zero-noise performance is a best case, not a forecast.** Real calibration at a new basin will have gauge error, incomplete or surrogate meteorological forcing, and model structural error that this phase's design deliberately excluded. Expect the real acceptable region to be broader than what's shown here, not the same size.
- **The specific value f=0.012 is not a validated physical anchor** — it was chosen as a free calibration parameter for this synthetic truth, not derived independently from soil survey data. There's no basis yet for assuming a new basin's f will be numerically similar, only that the *identifiability structure* (its interaction with Ks, the band's shape) is likely qualitatively similar if that basin is also infiltration-excess-dominated, which is the working assumption for semi-arid basins generally.
- **Whether the acceptable band's shape itself — not just its existence — is specific to this basin's terrain and soil setup is untested.** The curvature-vs-storm-magnitude relationship in §2.3, and the branch-elimination structure in §2.2, might depend on properties specific to this basin's mesh and soils and not reproduce numerically at a different site, even if the qualitative pattern holds. Testable once a second basin's synthetic-truth sweep exists, but not yet asked.
- **Whether parameters calibrated at one basin can be directly applied to a different one without recalibration is a separate, larger question** that this phase's single-basin design doesn't address at all — it's a question about regionalization across basins, not about identifiability within one.

### 3.4 Concrete recommendations for calibrating a new basin, following from this phase's evidence

1. **Before trusting any calibration result at a new basin, run the single cheapest check that caught this phase's biggest bug: score the pipeline once at a known point and confirm it reproduces the expected answer.** For a real basin there's no "true" parameter point to check against, but there are cheaper analogues — verifying the scoring pipeline reproduces a perfect score on a self-comparison (an observed record scored against itself), or checking any point where an independent hand-calculation is feasible — before trusting sweep-wide conclusions. The specific failure mode this guards against, described in full in §2.1, is a scoring pipeline that behaves *internally consistently* (better fits score better than worse fits) while still being *systematically wrong* in an absolute sense — the kind of bug that's invisible unless something with a known right answer is checked directly.
2. **Plan the calibration around multiple events from the outset, not a single best storm**, given how much tighter — and more honestly placed — the combined acceptable region was here relative to any single storm (§2.3).
3. **Report Ks/f results as a range, with the range's behavior across different f values characterized**, not as a bare point estimate — the biggest structural finding in this whole phase is that a single-number report would be misleading even under ideal conditions (§2.2).
4. **Treat the routing parameters (cv, r, n) as a separate, secondary calibration step from Ks/f, evaluated on phase-specific timing metrics computed at fine output resolution — not on the composite score.** The full workflow and evidence behind this is in §2.5: r is identifiable mainly through recession rate, n only through timing-specific metrics (and only if output resolution and sampled range are both wide enough to avoid a false null result), and cv should be expected to remain unconstrained by the data regardless of method, set from a reasonable prior rather than calibrated.
5. **Decompose the composite fit score into its parts as a standing step, not a one-off check.** §2.2's branch-elimination finding was invisible until the shape-matching term was examined on its own, and it cost nothing extra to compute since it's already part of the same sweep. Skipping this decomposition risks quietly accepting a spurious parameter region the way a volume-only view would have here.
6. **When data allows, re-run this phase's exact multi-event-narrowing test at the new basin** — even informally, comparing single- vs. multi-event acceptable regions — as the most direct test of whether the finding in §2.3 actually transfers, rather than assuming it does.

## 4. Methodological lessons worth carrying forward (not just results — practices)

These come directly out of what it took to get a trustworthy answer this phase, and are worth keeping as standing practice for the next basin, not just this one:

- **Scoring a known point is the cheapest sanity check on any pipeline, and should run first, not last.** It's what caught the resampling bug in §2.1, and it would catch an analogous bug in a different pipeline just as fast.
- **Raw, un-interpolated scatter diagnostics are the ground-truth check against contour-plot artifacts, not a nice-to-have.** Dense banding in interpolated contour surfaces has repeatedly turned out to be an artifact of how the interpolation handles very different-scaled axes (Ks spanning roughly 3–11, f spanning roughly 0.003–0.05), not a real feature of the underlying relationship. Generate both the interpolated and raw-scatter version every time; trust the raw version when they disagree.
- **A small, targeted spot-check before a full-scale re-run is worth the time.** An 8-point check correctly predicted the qualitative outcome of a much larger rescoring pass in a fraction of the compute.
- **"The numbers changed in the expected direction" is not sufficient verification on its own.** More than once this phase, a result that looked plausible turned out to need a second, independent check before it could be trusted. Both were only caught because someone asked *why* the number should look the way it did, not just whether it moved in the right direction.
- **Keep old numbers alongside corrected ones rather than overwriting them.** Every corrected results file in this project carries both the old (buggy) and new (corrected) values side by side, which is what made a stale-number catch possible earlier this phase, and what makes an audit trail like this document possible at all.
- **A composite score can hide a real disagreement between its own parts.** §2.2's branch-elimination finding only exists because the shape-matching term was checked on its own, not just folded into the composite score. A single number passing a threshold doesn't mean everything feeding into it agrees — decomposing a composite metric into its components should be routine, not something that only happens when a result looks unusual and prompts a second look.

It has not been tested at a different basin.
- **No second-basin synthetic or real test yet exists** to check whether any of §3's transferability expectations actually hold — that's the natural next step, not something this document can settle on its own.
